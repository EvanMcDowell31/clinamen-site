## The Charters Graph

Thirty-eight texts of rights, as a map to explore.

## How it works: Pick a text, a part or a right, or search

1. **Pick a text.** The map lights its links and the rights it names. The card gives its words, each link’s sentence with the passages it quotes, and a tree of its parts.
2. **Pick a part.** Any of 1,577 articles, sections, amendments and chapters. The map leads to the passages its links quote, in the texts they come from.
3. **Pick a right.** Any of 42, in six families, from speech, press and belief to government by consent. The map lights every text that names it, with a count.
4. **Search in any spelling.** Rights, texts, parts and passages. Type “choose” and it finds the parchment’s “chuse”; “cruel and unusual” finds 1689’s “cruell and unusuall”.

## Word for word: Each link rests on the words it quotes

A link says in one sentence how a later text drew on an earlier one, and most quote both texts word for word. The map pins each link to the passages it quotes, so the Eighth Amendment leads to the English Bill of Rights’ bail clause, the Body of Liberties’ liberties 42, 45 and 46, the Northwest Ordinance’s Article 2 and the Universal Declaration’s Article 5.

- **The very same words, tied.** The Bill of Rights’ ten amendments and the Constitution’s first ten. “Article the second”, proposed in 1789 and ratified in 1992 as the Twenty-seventh Amendment. Virginia’s bail clause of 1776 and the English Bill of Rights’ of 1689, spelling aside.
- **Rights found by their words.** A right is shown where its words are in a text’s own passages, not in headings or notes added to it, and the page shows the words that found each passage.
- **A reading, not legal advice.** The links and the rights are a reading of the texts. Each rests on the words shown, so you can weigh it yourself.

## Questions: What people ask first

**Is it legal advice?** No. Its links and the rights it finds are a reading of the texts, and each rests on the words it shows, so you can weigh it yourself. For a legal question, ask a lawyer.

**Where do the texts come from?** From the bodies that keep them, such as the National Archives, the United States Code, legislation.gov.uk and the United Nations, word for word, each checked against other copies.

**Can I use the texts?** Yes, on each text’s own terms, which its card on the map states and Irie’s [PROVENANCE.md](https://github.com/irie-people/irie/blob/main/PROVENANCE.md) lists. Give the credits above when you use the United Kingdom’s texts or Sweden’s Press Act.

**Do I need an account, or to install anything?** No. It is one web page that runs in your browser, on a phone or a computer, and loads nothing from elsewhere.

**What does it cost?** Nothing. It is free to use, and its code is open source under Apache-2.0, as part of Irie.
